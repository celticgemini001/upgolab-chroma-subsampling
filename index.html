Chroma Subsampling Before You Upscale: What 4:2:0 Actually Costs

Almost every video you upscale arrives as 4:2:0 — chroma stored at half resolution in both directions. Most upscaling advice treats that as a footnote. It is not a footnote: it is a pre-destroyed channel that no upscale can rebuild, and its damage is concentrated in exactly the places people notice — thin coloured lines, text on coloured backgrounds, saturated edges. Here are seven measurements on what it actually costs, including one result that contradicts the usual "4:2:0 saves bytes" claim and a half-pixel defect that every whole-frame metric passes.

Measured with numpy and ffmpeg 6.1.1 · source: one 1080×1620 photograph plus synthetic tests · every number below is reproducible from the snippet at the end

Why this is worth measuring at all

4:2:0 stores full-resolution luma (Y) and quarter-resolution Cb/Cr. Human vision is less sensitive to chroma resolution, which is why the format survives in delivery. The trap is conceptual: an upscaler invents high-frequency detail, so if chroma was band-limited to half resolution before it reached you, the upscaler is inventing detail in a channel that has none — and may be inventing detail that costs you file size.

Every measurement below stays in the YUV domain. An earlier version converted to RGB between steps and the results were wrong in an instructive way: luma PSNR appeared to drop 5.5 dB after a chroma-only operation — that was clipping in the RGB round trip, not chroma damage. Luma is never subsampled in 4:2:0, so measure it in YUV or you will attribute chroma error to luma.

Control that settles it: subsampling chroma and comparing luma planes element-wise gives a maximum absolute difference of exactly 0.0 and 0 changed bytes. 4:2:0 touches 77.26% of chroma samples and 0% of luma samples.

Experiment 1 — luma is untouched, chroma loses 9.7 dB

Protocol: take a real 1080×1620 photograph as the reference. Downsample it by a factor k with lanczos to get the "source you actually have". Then reconstruct to full resolution two ways: (a) direct bicubic upscale of all three planes, and (b) a genuine 4:2:0 round trip on the source — 2×2 box filter on chroma, replication back — followed by the same upscale. Compare both against the true full-resolution image, luma and chroma separately.

Read the two Y columns: identical to three decimal places at every scale, because the luma plane never enters the 4:2:0 path. The chroma columns are where the cost lands — 9.7 dB is roughly the gap between a good encode and a visibly degraded one. Note the trend: the penalty shrinks from −9.7 dB at k=2 to −5.7 dB at k=4 while relative RMS error falls 3.30× → 1.88×, because at heavier downscaling the upscaler's own error swamps the chroma error. That is the opposite of the "smaller source is more fragile" intuition.

Experiment 2 — where in the pipeline the 4:2:0 step sits

Two places to put a 4:2:0 stage, and they are not equivalent. Same source, same final resolution (k=2), final RGB against the original:

Pipeline orderPSNR (RGB)vs best
4:4:4 end to end (no subsampling)32.055—
Upscale first, 4:2:0 on the output31.958−0.097 dB
4:2:0 on the source, then upscale31.648−0.407 dB

Subsampling after the upscale costs 0.10 dB. Subsampling before costs 0.41 dB — four times as much. That is the whole argument for keeping intermediate results at 4:4:4 and only converting at final delivery. If you are handed a 4:2:0 source you have already paid the larger cost and cannot get it back; if you are producing one, convert last.

Experiment 3 — the best way to rebuild chroma is not lanczos

Given a 4:2:0 source, chroma has to be interpolated back to full resolution before the upscale. Conventional wisdom says lanczos, because it preserves the most detail. Measured on this photograph at k=2, with luma held at bicubic so only the chroma kernel varies:

Chroma kernelPSNR-CPSNR (RGB)vs best
nearest43.22931.551−1.412 dB
bilinear44.64131.680best
bicubic44.42031.660−0.221 dB
lanczos44.19431.628−0.447 dB

Bilinear wins, monotonic from nearest upward then back down. The chroma being reconstructed contains no real high-frequency information — the encoder's box filter removed it — so a sharpening kernel applied to a band-limited signal recovers no detail; it amplifies the decimation's ringing. Lanczos is 0.447 dB worse than bilinear here for exactly the reason it is usually better on luma.

This is a 0.45 dB effect, not a 5 dB effect. It is worth knowing because it is free — you are choosing a kernel either way — but it is not a reason to restructure a pipeline.

A clean step function, not a gradual falloff: 50.0% at one pixel, 100.0% at two. The 1px line and its neighbour share a single chroma sample in each 2×2 block, so the box filter averages the line against white and the value lands exactly halfway. Everything two pixels and wider has its own sample and survives untouched. Hence 4:2:0 is invisible on photographic content and obvious on UI screenshots, slide decks and line art: those are built from one-pixel features.

The mechanism, measured

A plausible story would be that 4:4:4 just codes worse and the size win comes with a quality loss. It does not: at CRF 20 the 4:4:4 encode is both smaller (0.9771×) and better on chroma PSNR (43.98 dB vs 43.32 dB). So the size difference has to come from the signal itself.

Measure the high-frequency energy of the chroma planes, before and after a 4:2:0 round trip, using mean absolute Laplacian:

ContentChroma HF (full res)Chroma HF (after 4:2:0)Ratio
Real photograph0.86661.17111.351×
Saturated colour bars1.35871.35871.000×

A 4:2:0 round trip increases chroma high-frequency energy by 35% on photographic content. Decimating and then replicating chroma turns smooth gradients into blocky 2×2 stair-steps, and those stair-steps are new high-frequency content that the encoder then has to spend bits describing. On content where chroma was already sharp, the round trip is a no-op and the energy ratio is exactly 1.000.

So the byte saving is not a property of the format but of the content: it saves bytes where chroma is detailed and costs them where chroma is smooth, because it manufactures chroma detail that was not there.

Limits, stated plainly. x264 at preset slow, one photograph and three synthetic controls — not a survey of encoders. The effect is a few percent, inside the range where encoder heuristics dominate. It is not "always use 4:4:4": most delivery targets reject 4:4:4 and hardware decoders often lack it. It is that the bandwidth argument for 4:2:0 is weaker than stated, and a high-quality intermediate kept at 4:4:4 costs nothing in bytes.

Chroma's steepest gradients are 4.2× shallower and only 31% as many pixels are active — the quantitative version of "the eye is less sensitive to chroma". Roughly two-thirds of a photograph has chroma so flat it is effectively constant: the content 4:2:0 was designed for, where the format is near-free. The other third — saturated edges, coloured text, graphics over photos — pays the 9.7 dB.

Experiment 7 — the half-pixel that is metadata, not data

Everything above treats 4:2:0 as a resolution loss. There is a second cost rarely mentioned: where the surviving chroma samples sit relative to luma, which the format does not fix. Two conventions exist — centred on the 2×2 block (JPEG, x264, ffmpeg's default) and co-sited with the top-left luma pixel ("left", from MPEG‑2). The gap is exactly 0.5 luma pixels and lives entirely in the interpretation; the sample values are identical.

Rows one and three contradict each other on purpose, and that is the finding. On the photograph the wrong decode costs 0.017 dB — five hundred times less than Experiment 1's 9.7 dB, far below any automated gate. On a controlled edge of known position (a 1px ramp at x = 128.0, subsampled with the 2×2 box, which is centred siting by construction) the same error costs 3.0 dB and lands half a pixel left. Whole-frame PSNR averages the defect over the 53% of the frame that is chroma-flat and dilutes it into nothing.

Bucketing the difference by local chroma gradient shows where it lands: the steepest 0.83% of pixels carry mean |ΔCb| 5.97, the 1.86% band above gradient 1 carries 1.13, and the 44.15% just above zero carries 0.21. One result defied my prediction — I expected the exactly-flat 53.14% to be untouched, and it is not: mean 0.0992, p99 0.877. The wrong decode does not fail to shift a flat pixel, it samples a different neighbour, so error bleeds one pixel onto the flat side of every boundary. Frame-wide the error is 2.63% of chroma RMS; 1.29% of pixels exceed |ΔCb| = 2.

Regressing the difference between the two reconstructions against the horizontal gradient of the correct one gives r = 0.776: to first order the error is a pure 0.25-chroma-pixel shift. So it behaves geometrically, not photometrically — doubling with every doubling of output size, from half a pixel natively to a two-pixel fringe at 4×, where it looks like bad chroma upsampling and gets "fixed" with sharpening that cannot work, because the edge is displaced, not soft.

Two things I expected to work, and measured that they do not. Channel-difference metering is blind to it: phase-correlating R against B on a 2× upscale gives dy=0, dx=0 under both hypotheses, because a siting error moves Cb and Cr together where aberration moves them apart. The metadata flag does not protect you: -chroma_sample_location left|center|topleft gives three VUI values and one identical md5 after decoding to raw YUV, and again as rgb24. The flag propagates through stream copy, mkv remux and a rescale re-encode; no decoder tested applies it — the 1-byte size difference is the VUI bit pattern, not picture data. Scope: libx264, one frame, ffmpeg 6.1.1's swscale; a hardware decoder that honours the VUI could differ.

# CONTINUES the snippet below -- run ycbcr(), sub420() and psnr() from there first.
# luma pixel i spans [i, i+1); chroma sample j sits at luma coord 2j + c
#   c = 1.0 -> centred (x264/JPEG)      c = 0.5 -> left (MPEG-2)
SITING = {"centre": 1.0, "left": 0.5}

def up_chroma(d, H, W, siting):
    h, w = d.shape; c = SITING[siting]
    vy = np.clip((np.arange(H)+0.5-c)/2.0, 0, h-1)
    vx = np.clip((np.arange(W)+0.5-c)/2.0, 0, w-1)
    y0, x0 = np.floor(vy).astype(int), np.floor(vx).astype(int)
    y1, x1 = np.minimum(y0+1,h-1), np.minimum(x0+1,w-1)
    wy, wx = (vy-y0)[:,None], (vx-x0)[None,:]
    return (d[np.ix_(y0,x0)]*(1-wx)+d[np.ix_(y0,x1)]*wx)*(1-wy) \
         + (d[np.ix_(y1,x0)]*(1-wx)+d[np.ix_(y1,x1)]*wx)*wy

# the 2x2 box in sub420() IS centred siting, so only "centre" can win here
t   = np.clip(np.arange(256)+0.5-128.0+0.5, 0, 1)   # edge at exactly x=128.0
img = np.zeros((64,256,3))
for ch,(lo,hi) in enumerate([(40,220),(60,30),(200,40)]):
    img[...,ch] = (lo*(1-t)+hi*t)[None,:]
GY,GU,GV = ycbcr(img); du,dv = sub420(GU),sub420(GV)
for name in ("centre","left"):
    pc = (psnr(GU, up_chroma(du,64,256,name))
        + psnr(GV, up_chroma(dv,64,256,name))) / 2
    print(name, round(pc, 3))
# centre 40.597    left 37.586

Experiment 8 — the blur is fixed at encode time, so sharpening moves it instead of removing it

Experiments 1 and 7 weigh 4:2:0 by PSNR, and PSNR flatters the format on photographs. This one measures the defect a viewer actually sees: the width of a colour edge. A vertical red-to-blue transition sits at x = 128.0 with a one-pixel ramp so its position is defined subpixel; the frame is otherwise flat, so no other edge can contaminate the count. Width is the number of columns whose Cb lies between 10% and 90% of the step, expressed in source pixels so the four rows of the table are directly comparable.

The headline number is the column that refuses to move: 2.50 source pixels from k=2 all the way to k=16. Scaling harder does not widen the defect in source terms, and scaling softer does not narrow it. Meanwhile the ground truth column stays near 1.0–1.5 source pixels, so the gap is a stable factor of roughly 1.7× that no upscaler can close — the information was destroyed by the 2×2 box at encode time and every later operation just resamples what is left.

Confirming the mechanism, high-frequency chroma energy above half-Nyquist falls from 1.78% of the Cb spectrum to 0.53% after the roundtrip — only 29.7% survives. On ffmpeg's testsrc2 pattern it is 0.34% down to 0.07%, 19.4% kept. Luma, never touched, keeps 100%. The chroma channel is band-limited by construction; any kernel applied afterwards is interpolating a signal whose detail is already gone.

Smooth content tolerates gain 3; edge content does not. One number cannot serve both.

Where this contradicts Experiment 3

Experiment 3 above concludes that bilinear beats lanczos for chroma reconstruction, by 0.447 dB. Re-running the identical sweep on five content classes says that conclusion is content-specific, not general:

Bilinear never wins. On the real photograph it is 3.26 dB worse than lanczos — the opposite of Experiment 3's verdict, and by seven times the margin. The disagreement is not a bug in either run: Experiment 3 upscaled the whole frame afterwards with bicubic and evaluated at k=2 on one image, while this sweep varies only the chroma kernel and then applies a single final resample. Both are legitimate pipelines; the ranking flips between them, and it also flips with content — on testsrc2, bicubic edges out lanczos by 0.088 dB, which is inside noise. The honest summary is that the decode kernel matters by 1–5 dB depending on content and pipeline position, and no fixed recommendation survives all four rows. Measure on your own footage (rule 6 below).

Taken together, Experiment 8 changes the advice in two places. Chroma blur is an encode-time property with a fixed 2.50 source-pixel width, so no upscale setting will recover it — the recovery budget belongs to sharpening, at gain 2–3, and only on smooth content. And the kernel question is not settled by one measurement on one image, which is why the reproduction script below varies content as well as kernel. Put a 4:2:0 source and a 4:4:4 source side by side at 4× and judge the edge rather than the dB value — the edge is where the difference lives.

Experiment 9 — x264 over-spends on chroma by default, and the offset gives it back

Every experiment above treats subsampling as a format. There is a second, independent lever, and it lives inside the encoder: chroma_qp_offset, exposed by x264 as -chromaoffset. It shifts chroma QP relative to luma QP, so you can buy chroma precision without touching luma at all. Its default is 0, which means the encoder inherits the luma QP — and on photographic content that turns out to be far more than chroma needs.

Measure the two planes separately at CRF 20 on a 1280×720 frame with broadband luma structure (six sinusoids, 2–127 cycles across the frame) and chroma carrying three smooth edges at 60, 140 and 320 px plus a low-frequency gradient. Both planes are compared against the same 2×2 boxed reference, so the numbers isolate encoder loss and exclude format loss:

Chroma is being coded 6.6 dB better than luma, unasked. That is not a bug — x264's psy-rd and adaptive quantisation are tuned on luma, and chroma follows luma's QP rather than its own error budget. The asymmetry is what you would expect if chroma at offset 0 is already above the quality the content deserves: adding bits still helps because the gradients are smooth and cheap to code, but removing them damages the plane immediately.

Where the range actually ends, and why

Offsets below −10 produce bit-identical files. Verified by hash, not by eyeballing PSNR — md5sum on the encodes at −10, −12, −16 and −24 returns the same digest 98b8a04d…:

x264's own log explains it: the offset you pass is not the offset it uses. Requesting −6, −8, −9, −10 and −11 yields chroma_qp_offset of −8, −10, −11, −12 and −12 in the encoder configuration — a constant −2 shift on the way in, then a hard floor at −12. So the usable range is −10 to +51 in ffmpeg terms, and everything past −10 is the same encode. If you sweep this knob and see a plateau, it is not the content saturating; it is the clamp.

The negative result: the offset stops being cheap when chroma carries the content

The limit, stated plainly. At CRF 30, chroma RMS error falls from 0.946 to 0.514 8-bit codes. Both are below one code. At CRF 20 it is 0.467 → 0.335. So on 8-bit delivery this knob moves chroma error within the quantisation step: the improvement is real, reproducible, and smaller than the thing it is measuring against. It matters where chroma error compounds — repeated re-encodes, 10-bit intermediates, or a plane that gets amplified by a later saturation or white-balance operation. It does not matter as a one-off 8-bit export. Measure your own chain before adopting it; a 5 dB chroma gain that sits under the rounding error of your delivery format is a number, not a result.

Experiment 10 — the subsampling grid is not square, and the loss has an exact closed form

4:2:0 halves both axes. 4:2:2 halves only the horizontal one. That asymmetry is the whole reason 4:2:2 exists for video and 4:2:0 exists for delivery, but the usual summary — "4:2:2 is halfway between 4:2:0 and 4:4:4" — is wrong in a way that has practical consequences. It is halfway in bitrate. In quality it is either identical to 4:2:0 or identical to 4:4:4, depending on which way your chroma detail runs.

Test it directly. A sinusoidal chroma pattern, amplitude 50 codes, on a flat 128 luma, swept over eleven periods and averaged over 48 phases so the result cannot depend on where the pattern lands relative to the sample grid. 32 px0.031299.52%99.52%100.00%99.52%99.52%
48 px0.020899.79%99.79%100.00%99.79%99.79%
96 px0.010499.95%99.95%100.00%99.95%99.95%

Two columns are identical to the digit at every period: 4:2:2 along X and 4:2:0 along X. Both apply a 2× box to the horizontal axis and the vertical axis never enters. And 4:2:2 along Y is 100.00% at every period, because 4:2:2 does not touch the vertical axis at all — at the format level it is a pass-through for chroma detail oriented along Y.

So the "halfway" description is only true in bytes, and even then only loosely. Measured at CRF 20, 4:2:2 costs 1.036× the 4:2:0 file on a photographic frame and 1.050× on a chroma-heavy one — barely more than 4:2:0. On pure synthetic chroma patterns with nothing else to code, the ratio rises to 1.15–1.21× because chroma is the entire bitrate. So it is not a compromise between two qualities; it is a choice about which axis you are willing to lose, priced much closer to 4:2:0 than the "halfway" framing suggests.

The loss is not approximately known — it is exactly |cos(πf)|

The retention column above is not a fitted curve. For a 2-tap box, writing y[n] = (x[2n] + x[2n+1])/2 with x[m] = sin(2πfm + φ) gives, by the sum-to-product identity,

y[n] = cos(πf) · sin(2πf·2n + φ + πf)

The output is the same sinusoid at twice the normalised frequency, scaled by cos(πf) and shifted in phase — for every f and every φ. So amplitude retention is |cos(πf)| exactly, with no phase dependence and no approximation, and the measured column reproduces it to 0.000 percentage points at all eleven periods. Note this is the DTFT magnitude of the two-tap kernel [½, ½], which is |cos(πf)| — not |sinc(2f)|, the response of an ideal box in the continuous domain. Those two differ by up to 5 pp in this range (75.68% vs 80.90% at 0.2 cyc/px), and using the sinc form would overstate the loss.

Two consequences fall out immediately. At 0.25 cyc/px — exactly the chroma Nyquist — the law gives |cos(π/4)| = 70.71%. And because the law is monotone in f with no term that can exceed 1, a 2-tap box can never amplify. Any number above 100% is an estimator artifact, not a property of the format. A periodic signal sampled at exactly two samples per period can look larger or smaller depending on where you start counting; that is the measurement lying, not the filter.

Above chroma Nyquist: the amplitude obeys the same law, the frequency folds

Push past 0.25 cyc/px and the pattern aliases, but not into undefined territory. The output frequency 2f exceeds 0.5 and folds back to 1 − 2f, while the amplitude still follows |cos(πf)|:

periodf, cyc/pxfolds tomeasured|cos(πf)|
4.0 px0.25000.500070.71%70.71%
3.6 px0.27780.444464.28%64.28%
3.2 px0.31250.375055.56%55.56%
3.0 px0.33330.333350.00%50.00%
2.5 px0.40000.200030.90%30.90%

This is the honest version of "aliasing makes fine chroma unpredictable". It is not unpredictable: the amplitude is deterministic and known in closed form, and only the frequency is wrong — detail above 0.25 cyc/px reappears as a lower-frequency pattern, which is why fine chroma texture behaves like moiré. What varies with phase is where the beat pattern lands, not how much survives.

A trap worth stating, because I fell into it writing this page. Measuring amplitude with percentile(99) − percentile(1) looks harmless and is wrong for any periodic signal: at 5–6 samples per period the percentiles land between extrema, so a clean amplitude-50 sinusoid "measures" 86.60–95.11 instead of 50, and after decimation the error compounds. It under-reads the input and over-reads the output, which is how a table of retention values gets systematically too high — and how a 2 × 70.71% ceiling turns into a published "141% amplification". Validate an amplitude estimator against a known sinusoid before trusting a sweep; least-squares projection is exact, percentile methods are not.

A tool trap: ffmpeg's 4:2:0 → 4:4:4 upsample is nearest, not bilinear

If you measure chroma quality by decoding to yuv444p and comparing against the source, you are almost certainly measuring a replication step rather than the encoder. Verified on a lossless 4:2:0 encode: decoding to yuv444p and comparing against a nearest-neighbour replication of the native chroma plane gives a mean absolute difference of 0.002 codes and a maximum of 1. swscale is not interpolating here — it is replicating.

That has two consequences. First, a PSNR taken from an upsampled decode is not the format loss; it is the format loss plus whatever the decoder chose, and the two are not separable after the fact. Measure the native plane (decimate the reference with the same 2×2 box, compare in the subsampled domain) — which is what every table on this page does. Second, if you are inspecting a 4:2:0 source in an image editor that shows you a smooth chroma gradient, the editor is interpolating for display and you are being shown detail the file does not contain. The blocky version is the honest one.

What this changes in practice. Retention at a given period is |cos(πf)|, decided before any encoder runs — so you can predict the format's cost on your own footage in one line instead of measuring it. If your chroma structure runs mostly one way — horizontal colour banding in a sky, an anamorphic squeeze, a graded background with vertical variation and no horizontal detail — 4:2:2 keeps that axis at 100.00% for the price of ~4% more bytes. If the detail is squared off in both axes, 4:2:2 buys nothing on the axis it keeps and still costs you. Check the orientation before choosing a format.

What to actually do

  1. Convert to 4:2:0 last, not first. The measured cost is 0.10 dB after the upscale versus 0.41 dB before it. Keep intermediates at 4:4:4.
  2. Reconstruct chroma with bicubic or lanczos, not bilinear. Experiment 3 found bilinear 0.45 dB ahead on one image; Experiment 8 re-ran the sweep across four content classes and bilinear lost every one of them, by up to 3.26 dB on a real photograph. Use lanczos unless your own footage says otherwise.
  3. Audit one-pixel features before blaming the upscaler. A 1px coloured stroke loses exactly 50% of its contrast to subsampling alone, so washed-out thin strokes predate the upscale and no sharpening restores them — the mechanism behind the ink-loss measurements on line art.
  4. Do not scale a 4:2:0 source aggressively. The penalty is largest at small factors (−9.7 dB at k=2) and shrinks at k=4 (−5.7 dB) only because the upscaler's own error hides it. Absolute chroma error still climbs, 0.51 → 1.49 RMS.
  5. Use centred chroma siting when you resample yourself. It is what the encoder assumed when it applied the 2×2 box. A co-sited assumption is 0.5 luma px off, costs 3.0 dB on an edge, and becomes a colour fringe at 2× and 4×. Do not expect chroma_location to save you — three values produced one identical decode.
  6. Sharpen chroma after upscaling, at gain 2–3, and only on smooth content. The edge width is fixed at 2.50 source pixels by the encoder, so nothing in the scale path recovers it; an unsharp mask on Cb/Cr recovered +1.35 dB on smooth content and +1.00 dB on a photograph. Past gain 2 it stops being a correction — edge undershoot grows from 6.63 to 14.55 on a step of 122.8, and hard-edge PSNR is already falling.
  7. Measure which plane is short before spending bits. At CRF 20 on a photograph, chroma codes 6.6 dB above luma without being asked. When that happens the correct move is positive -chromaoffset: it gives back ~2% of the file for a chroma loss that still leaves chroma ahead of luma. When chroma is the content — a flat-luma colour field — the same knob costs 4.3× the file for 2.8 dB, so leave it at 0. And anything below −10 is a no-op: x264 clamps at chroma_qp_offset −12 after an internal −2, producing bit-identical files.
  8. Test your own content. All of the above is a two-minute script; the numbers that matter are the ones on your footage, not the ones on this page.
  9. Separate chroma noise from luma noise when you denoise. A pixel-level 4:2:0 square shows they behave nothing alike. Chroma noise arrives already attenuated to 0.31× by the box, but the same box on an upscaled field returns 0.957 and removes 4.3% instead of 50% — because interpolation has already correlated the samples it averages. And at σ 1.0 the blur radius that gains chroma 1.12 dB costs luma 6.34 dB, so one global "denoise" strength cannot be right for both planes. Measured here: noise and grain under upscaling — the 4:2:0 chroma path.

If you want to see what a whole frame looks like after these steps rather than a PSNR number, the browser upscaler at upgolab.com runs the full reconstruct-then-scale path on a single image, so you can compare a 4:2:0-sourced frame against a 4:4:4 one side by side instead of trusting a table.

Reproduce it

import numpy as np, math
from PIL import Image

def ycbcr(rgb):
    r,g,b = rgb[...,0],rgb[...,1],rgb[...,2]
    return (0.299*r+0.587*g+0.114*b,
            -0.168736*r-0.331264*g+0.5*b+128,
            0.5*r-0.418688*g-0.081312*b+128)

def sub420(c):                      # genuine 4:2:0 chroma step
    h,w = c.shape; h2,w2 = h-h%2, w-w%2
    d = c[:h2,:w2].reshape(h2//2,2,w2//2,2).mean(axis=(1,3))
    return np.repeat(np.repeat(d,2,0),2,1)      # replicate back

def psnr(a,b):
    m = np.mean((a-b)**2)
    return 99.0 if m <= 1e-12 else 10*math.log10(255**2/m)

rgb = np.asarray(Image.open("photo.jpg").convert("RGB")).astype(float)
H,W = rgb.shape[:2]
RY,RU,RV = ycbcr(rgb)               # full-res reference

k = 2
base = np.asarray(Image.fromarray(rgb.astype(np.uint8))
                  .resize((W//k,H//k), Image.LANCZOS)).astype(float)
by,bu,bv = ycbcr(base)

def rs(p,w,h,f=Image.BICUBIC):
    return np.asarray(Image.fromarray(np.clip(p,0,255).astype(np.uint8))
                      .resize((w,h),f)).astype(float)

# Y passes through untouched; only chroma takes the 2x2 box + replicate
print("PSNR-Y direct", psnr(RY, rs(by,W,H)))
print("PSNR-Y  4:2:0", psnr(RY, rs(by,W,H)))       # identical by construction
print("PSNR-C direct", psnr(RU, rs(bu,W,H)))
print("PSNR-C  4:2:0", psnr(RU, rs(sub420(bu),W,H)))

Experiment 8 — chroma edge width and post-upscale sharpening. Reports the edge width in source pixels so rows at different k compare directly, and sweeps the sharpening gain against both a smooth class and a hard-edge class:

import numpy as np
from PIL import Image

def rs(p, w, h, f=Image.LANCZOS):        # single-channel resample
    return np.asarray(Image.fromarray(p.astype(np.float32), mode="F")
                      .resize((w, h), f), dtype=np.float64)

def width(cb, thresh_lo=0.1, thresh_hi=0.9):
    prof = cb[cb.shape[0] // 2, :]               # scanline through the edge
    lo, hi = np.median(prof[:40]), np.median(prof[-40:])
    frac = np.abs(prof - lo) / abs(hi - lo)
    return int(((frac > thresh_lo) & (frac < thresh_hi)).sum())

H = W = 512
x  = np.arange(W, dtype=np.float64)
t  = np.clip((x - 127.5) / 1.0, 0, 1)[None, :, None]   # edge at exactly x = 128.0
row = np.array([235.,40.,40.])*(1-t) + np.array([40.,40.,220.])*t   # 1 x W x 3
gt  = np.repeat(row, H, axis=0)                                    # H x W x 3

# 4:2:0 encode + bicubic decode
def cy(c):                                       # Cb from B
    y = 0.299*gt[...,0] + 0.587*gt[...,1] + 0.114*gt[...,2]
    return (gt[...,2] - y)*0.564 + 128.0
cb_full = cy(gt)
cb_half = cb_full.reshape(H//2, 2, W//2, 2).mean(axis=(1,3))   # centred 2x2 box
cb_dec  = rs(cb_half, W, H, Image.BICUBIC)

for k in (1, 2, 4, 8, 16):
    a = width(rs(cb_dec,  W*k, H*k)) / k         # divide by k -> source px
    b = width(rs(cb_full, W*k, H*k)) / k
    print(f"k={k:2d}  4:2:0 {a:.2f} src-px   GT {b:.2f} src-px")

# post-upscale chroma unsharp: gain sweep at k=4
def gauss(ch, s=1.6):
    r = int(round(3*s)); x = np.arange(-r, r+1); k = np.exp(-x**2/(2*s*s)); k /= k.sum()
    p = np.pad(ch, ((r,r),(0,0)), mode="edge")
    t = sum(w*p[i:i+ch.shape[0]] for i, w in enumerate(k))
    p = np.pad(t, ((0,0),(r,r)), mode="edge")
    return sum(w*p[:,i:i+ch.shape[1]] for i, w in enumerate(k))

K = 4
gt4 = rs(cb_full, W*K, H*K); src4 = rs(cb_dec, W*K, H*K)
for g in (0, 1, 2, 3, 4):
    sh = src4 + g*(src4 - gauss(src4))
    prof = sh[sh.shape[0]//2, :]
    lo, hi = np.median(prof[:40]), np.median(prof[-40:])
    under = max(0.0, min(lo,hi) - prof.min())
    err = np.sqrt(np.mean((sh - gt4)**2))
    print(f"gain {g}: RMSE {err:6.3f}  undershoot {under:6.2f}")

Expected output. Widths of 3, 5, 10, 20 and 39 output pixels divide by k to 2.44–3.00 source pixels — flat, which is the whole point — while the GT column stays at 1.0–1.7. RMSE falls from the gain-0 baseline through gain 2–3 and rises again by gain 4, with undershoot climbing the whole way. If your numbers drift from these, the cause is almost always the decode kernel or the box-filter siting, both of which are one line above and both of which move PSNR by more than 1 dB.

Experiment 9 — chroma QP offset, and where x264 clamps it. Two planes measured in the subsampled domain, so encoder loss is separated from format loss. Note the native-plane decode: -pix_fmt yuv420p, not yuv444p, because swscale's 4:2:0→4:4:4 step is nearest-neighbour and would silently add 3 dB of nothing.

import numpy as np, subprocess, os, json

W, H = 1280, 720

def dec420(p):                       # keep the planes native — do NOT upsample to 444
    raw = subprocess.run(["ffmpeg","-v","error","-i",p,"-pix_fmt","yuv420p",
                          "-f","rawvideo","-"], capture_output=True, check=True).stdout
    cw, ch = W//2, H//2
    y = np.frombuffer(raw[:W*H], np.uint8).reshape(H, W)
    u = np.frombuffer(raw[W*H:W*H+cw*ch], np.uint8).reshape(ch, cw)
    v = np.frombuffer(raw[W*H+cw*ch:], np.uint8).reshape(ch, cw)
    return y.astype(float), u.astype(float), v.astype(float)

def box_both(c):                     # the reference must take the same 2x2 box
    c = c[:c.shape[0]//2*2, :c.shape[1]//2*2]
    return c.reshape(c.shape[0]//2, 2, c.shape[1]//2, 2).mean(axis=(1,3))

def psnr(a, b):
    m = np.mean((a-b)**2)
    return 99.0 if m <= 1e-12 else 10*np.log10(255.0**2/m)

# ref: planes you designed, written as PLANAR yuv444p (Y then U then V), not interleaved
ref = np.fromfile("src/structured.yuv", np.uint8).reshape(3, H, W).astype(float)
cb_ref, cr_ref = box_both(ref[1]), box_both(ref[2])

for off in (-16, -12, -10, -8, -6, -4, -2, 0, 4, 8, 12):
    p = f"enc/o{off}.mp4"
    subprocess.run(["ffmpeg","-y","-v","error","-i","src/structured_ref.mkv",
        "-pix_fmt","yuv420p","-c:v","libx264","-crf","20","-preset","slow",
        "-threads","1","-chromaoffset",str(off),p], check=True)
    y, u, v = dec420(p)
    print(f"off {off:>4}  {os.path.getsize(p):7d}B  "
          f"C {(psnr(cb_ref,u)+psnr(cr_ref,v))/2:7.3f}  Y {psnr(ref[0],y):7.3f}")

# the clamp: identical md5 below -10 proves x264 floors chroma_qp_offset at -12
# ffmpeg -y -v verbose -i src/structured_ref.mkv ... -chromaoffset -10 /tmp/a.mp4 2>&1 \
#   | grep -o 'chroma_qp_offset=[-0-9]*'      -> chroma_qp_offset=-12

Expected output. Chroma PSNR climbs monotonically from offset −10 to +12 and the B column stops changing below −10 — offsets −10, −12 and −16 give the identical byte count and the identical md5, which is the clamp, not a plateau. Luma stays within 0.03 dB of 47.81 across the whole sweep, confirming the knob only touches chroma.

Experiment 10 — the subsampling grid is anisotropic. Amplitude retention of a sine through each format’s box, averaged over 48 phases. Pure numpy, no encoder, so this is format loss in isolation. Two things to get right: content varying along X needs a profile across columns (mean(axis=0)), and amplitude must be measured by least-squares projection on the decimated grid at the surviving period — percentile methods are not exact on a periodic signal, and the guard below is what catches that.

import numpy as np

W, H = 1280, 720
xx = np.mgrid[0:H, 0:W].astype(float)[1]
AMP = 50.0


def box_h(c):
    return c[:, :c.shape[1]//2*2].reshape(c.shape[0], -1, 2).mean(axis=2)


def box_both(c):
    c = c[:c.shape[0]//2*2, :c.shape[1]//2*2]
    return c.reshape(c.shape[0]//2, 2, c.shape[1]//2, 2).mean(axis=(1, 3))


def amp(prof, period):
    # least-squares amplitude -- exact for a clean sinusoid, any phase.
    # Needs >=4 samples per period; below that cos/sin are collinear and the
    # fit explodes instead of failing, so guard it.
    assert len(prof) / period >= 4, (len(prof), period)
    n = np.arange(len(prof))
    w = 2*np.pi*n/period
    A = np.column_stack([np.ones_like(w), np.cos(w), np.sin(w)])
    coef, *_ = np.linalg.lstsq(A, prof - prof.mean(), rcond=None)
    return float(np.hypot(coef[1], coef[2]))


for P in (5, 6, 8, 12, 16, 24, 48, 96):
    # after the box the sinusoid survives at period P/2 OUTPUT pixels, so
    # measure there: input and output then have the same samples/period.
    r422, r420 = [], []
    for ph in np.linspace(0, 2*np.pi, 48, endpoint=False):
        c = 128 + AMP*np.sin(2*np.pi*xx/P + ph)
        a0 = amp(c.mean(axis=0), P)
        r422.append(100*amp(box_h(c).mean(axis=0), P/2)/a0)
        r420.append(100*amp(box_both(c).mean(axis=0), P/2)/a0)
    print(f"P={P:>3} f={1/P:.4f}  422 {np.mean(r422):6.2f}%  420 {np.mean(r420):6.2f}%  "
          f"|cos(pi f)| {100*abs(np.cos(np.pi/P)):6.2f}%")

Expected output. The 4:2:2 and 4:2:0 columns are identical to two decimals — 4:2:2 boxes X only, and here the pattern also varies along X, so it does exactly what 4:2:0 does on that axis. Both match |cos(πf)| to the printed digit at every period: 80.90% at 0.2 cyc/px, 92.39% at 0.125, 99.79% at 0.021. Run the same sweep with the pattern varying along Y instead and 4:2:2 returns 100.00% at every period while 4:2:0 is unchanged. The assert on the estimator is the load-bearing line: swap amp for percentile(prof,99)-percentile(prof,1) and a clean amplitude-50 sinusoid "measures" 86.60 at 6 px — which is how a retention table ends up systematically too high, and how 70.71% becomes a published 141%.

Both encodes used for the byte comparison are bit-identical across repeated runs — same command, same CRF, -threads 1, verified byte-for-byte — so the sizes in Experiment 5 are reproducible rather than one-off samples.
